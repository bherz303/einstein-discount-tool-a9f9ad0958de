Discount & Escalation Playbook

What to do when a customer says the move took too long — investigate first, then find fair ground.

Decision Tool Scenarios & Strategies
Company-Wide Manager Reference
⚠️ Hard rule: A manager can offer up to $500 on their own authority (must provide a write-up for the decision). Any discount above that must be reviewed with your Branch Manager and Regional Manager (RM) or the CET (Customer Experience Team Lead) before it goes to the customer. It's the dollar amount that matters, not the percentage — a $300 discount on a $600 move is fine; a $700 discount on a $4,000 move needs a sign-off.

The Genius Response — 9 Steps

Run every discount conversation through this
1
Listen to everything first
Let the customer say everything they need to say before you respond with facts or a fix. Don't interrupt with an explanation — that reads as defensive, not helpful.
2
Investigate everything
Pull the contract. Look up the customer and the job in the system. See who was on the crew. Compare the original estimate, the on-site estimate, and the actual move time. Find out if there was an unexpected delay — disassembly, tight doorways, stairs, anything the crew had to work around.
3
Apologize — no matter what
Apologize for the frustration regardless of whose "fault" it turns out to be. The customer had a bad experience; that's worth acknowledging before anything else. This isn't admitting we did something wrong — it's acknowledging how they feel.
4
Set the follow-up expectation
Tell the customer you'll talk to your crew and follow up the next day once you've heard their side. This buys time to actually investigate instead of negotiating blind on the phone.
5
If pricing was fair, say so — with facts
If the investigation shows the pricing and time were fair, take that information back to the customer and walk them through it. Explain everything — the estimates, the actual time, the delay causes. Don't lead with a discount just to make the call end.
6
If they still push back, land on a fair dollar amount
If the customer still doesn't want to pay after hearing the facts, offer a specific, fixed dollar amount that fits the situation. "I can take $X off for you." You can use a percentage to sanity-check that it's proportional, but the number you commit to is always a fixed dollar figure — never open-ended. A mistake is the same size whether it's a one-bedroom or a five-bedroom. Land on the dollars.
7
Extreme measure — split the difference
If we went over our own quote, a good tactic in extreme situations is splitting the difference between the quoted amount and the actual charge with the customer. This is reserved for real overage situations, not general complaints.
8
Over $500 — stop and escalate
If getting to a fair resolution means going over $500 — even with the split-the-difference tactic — do not offer it yourself. Pause the conversation and review it with your Branch Manager and RM or the CET first, with a write-up of the decision. This is a hard rule, not a judgment call. Set the same next-day follow-up expectation with the customer while you get sign-off.
9
Last resort — charge it to the game
If all the steps above fail — and the resolution is within your $500 authority, or has been approved by your BM and RM / CET — take what you can get and move on. One difficult resolution isn't worth burning hours chasing the last dollar.

Worked Example — "The Movers Took Too Long"

Trainer Scenario
Original Estimate 6.0 hrs
On-Site Estimate 5.5 hrs
Actual Time 5.0 hrs

ListenCustomer says the move flat-out took too long and wants something done about it.

InvestigateCrew had to remove both refrigerator doors to get it through the new place's doorway, and fully disassemble an IKEA platform bed with multiple screws and parts — both legitimate, unplanned time costs.
ApologizeStill apologize for the frustration and how long the day felt, even though the numbers are in our favor.
Follow upTell the customer you're talking to the crew and will call back tomorrow with the full picture.
Conclusion: Pricing was fair — the crew beat both estimates despite extra disassembly work. This isn't a "give a discount to make it go away" situation. Take the facts back to the customer: original estimate, on-site estimate, actual time, and exactly what caused the extra labor (fridge doors, bed disassembly). If they still push back after hearing it straight, a small fixed goodwill dollar amount is reasonable — but nothing more, because the math already favors us.

Quick Scripts — What To Actually Say

1
The Apology Opener I'm really sorry the move took longer than you expected — that's frustrating no matter what caused it. Let me pull everything up and figure out exactly what happened.
2
Setting the Follow-Up I want to talk to the crew directly before I give you an answer I can stand behind. I'll call you back tomorrow with the full picture — I promise you won't be waiting on this.
3
Explaining the Facts Your original estimate was 6 hours, the on-site walkthrough brought it to 5.5, and the crew actually finished in 5 — under both. The extra effort was pulling both fridge doors and disassembling the bed, which the crew handled without adding time.
4
Framing a % Discount I hear you, and I want this to feel fair. I can take $X off for you. That's the best I can do while still being fair to the crew who worked hard on this.
5
Splitting the Difference We did go over our quote here. Instead of you covering the full overage, let's split it — you pay half of the difference between the quote and the actual charge. Extreme measures only — reserve for real overages, not general dissatisfaction.
6
When It's Going Over $500 To the customer: "I want to make this right, and I need to run this by my manager before I can give you a number — I'll have an answer for you by tomorrow." To yourself: this is not your call to make solo, get your BM and RM / CET on it now, with a write-up.
7
Charge It to the Game If it's within your $500 authority — or already approved by your BM and RM / CET — and the customer is still unhappy after everything, take the resolution and move on. Don't relitigate a $40 gesture that closed the account clean.
