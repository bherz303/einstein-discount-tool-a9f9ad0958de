🥇 The Golden Rule — Extra Movers Day-Of

When we have to send an extra mover the day of, we never use the wording "we'll give you a mover for free" or "a percentage of their time off." We always pick a dollar amount that makes sense for the situation and offer that. Estimate the extra mover's time and rate to get to a fair figure (e.g. ~4 hrs × $80/hr ≈ $320), then commit to that fixed dollar amount — so it's never open-ended if the day runs long.

Working draft. These five scenarios are starting points pulled from the most common patterns. The plan is to collect real situations from BMs across all branches, pool them into these themes, and sharpen the strategies with actual examples. If you've handled a case worth adding, send it to your RM.

Common Scenarios

1

"The move took too long / went over the estimate"

The classic. Customer feels the crew was slow or the bill came in high.
Investigate first
  • Pull the contract: original estimate, on-site estimate, and what was actually charged.
  • Compare against actual time and mover count. Did we beat the on-site estimate, or go over?
  • Talk to the crew — was there disassembly, tight access, extra items, or a real delay?
How to solve

Apologize for the frustration regardless. If the numbers were fair (at or under the on-site estimate), take the facts back and explain them plainly — don't lead with a discount. If we genuinely went over the on-site estimate without a good reason, lean toward covering that overage. If there was a legit reason, explain it and, if they still push, split the difference on the overage.

Where to land

A dollar amount tied to the overage — not a blanket % off the whole move. If the bill beat the estimate, the answer may be $0 plus a clear explanation.

2

"We had to send extra movers day-of"

The most important one to get right. See the Golden Rule above.
Investigate first
  • Why did we need the extra mover — our miscalculation, or a scope change on the customer's side?
  • How long was the extra mover actually on the job, and at what rate?
How to solve

If the extra mover was needed because we under-scoped the job, that time is on us. Estimate the extra mover's hours × rate to get a fair figure, then pick a fixed dollar amount. Never phrase it as "a free mover" or "a percentage of their time" — that reads as open-ended and can balloon if the day runs long.

Where to land

A fixed dollar amount ≈ estimated extra-mover hours × hourly rate (e.g. 4 hrs × $80 = $320). Use the Decision Tool's extra-mover helper to calculate it.

3

"Something was damaged"

Property or item damage — a claim may already be in motion.
Investigate first
  • Is a damage claim already filed? If so, this is not a discount you decide alone.
  • What's the extent of the damage, and did the crew flag it on site or note pre-existing damage?
How to solve

Coordinate with the CET / Claims before offering anything. The claim payout and any goodwill discount need to be decided together as one total resolution — not stacked as two separate numbers. Any discount here is for the inconvenience/experience, separate from what the claim covers for the actual damage.

Where to land

Decided with the CET / Claims. Flag it in the Decision Tool (Damage Claim Status) so the total resolution is coordinated.

4

"The crew's attitude / communication was off"

A values breakdown, even when the time and price were fine.
Investigate first
  • Which CHOICES value(s) did we miss — communication, attitude, attention to detail?
  • Get the crew's side. This is as much a coaching signal as a customer-resolution question.
How to solve

Apologize sincerely — this isn't the standard we hold ourselves to. The customer resolution (a modest goodwill dollar amount) is separate from the internal follow-up: log the values miss and coach the crew. Don't let a pricing discount stand in for the coaching conversation that actually needs to happen.

Where to land

A modest goodwill dollar amount for the experience, plus a documented coaching follow-up with the crew.

5

"Loyal / repeat customer with a minor gripe"

Small issue, but a relationship worth protecting.
Investigate first
  • Is the complaint valid, or is it more about feeling heard? Both matter.
  • What's the customer's history with us — repeat mover, referrer, reviewer?
How to solve

Lead with listening and a genuine thank-you for their loyalty. For a genuinely minor issue, a small fixed goodwill gesture goes a long way and protects a high-value relationship. Keep it proportional — this is about goodwill, not fixing a real service failure.

Where to land

A small fixed goodwill dollar amount, framed as a thank-you for their loyalty.

Teaching Lesson — A Real Case

The Open-Ended Cap

A real service-recovery call, anonymized — and how the tool would have changed it.
What happened

A repeat customer who'd had a poor prior move was booked for a 3-mover / 5-hour job (15 man-hours) and quoted a total of $1,764. Scheduling forced a last-minute branch transfer, the crew arrived 2-for-3 (understaffed), and — because a booking rep had waved the customer off when she tried to give an inventory — the job was badly under-scoped. On site the crew re-estimated ~20 man-hours. It ultimately ran 37.5 man-hours on a real mix of our failures (understaffing, no inventory) and day-of conditions (8th-floor logistics, a tiny destination unit, several people underfoot, the customer being very particular — and, near the end, the crew's read that she was milking the clock).

Quoted Total$1,764
Actual Invoice$3,825
Discount Given$2,061
Est → Actual20 → 37.5 hrs
Where it went wrong

The manager committed to honoring the original $1,764 quote — which quietly turned it into an open-ended cap. The discount wasn't a number he chose; it was whatever the gap happened to be ($3,825 − $1,764 = $2,061). He locked that in when the expectation was ~20 man-hours, so every additional hour the job drifted — including customer-caused hours — grew our exposure. Service recovery was warranted; the way the number was set was not. It was also more than 4× what a manager should approve alone, decided solo on the phone.

How this tool would have changed the call
  • No open-ended cap. The tool only ever produces a fixed dollar discount chosen from the facts known at that moment — it never lets you peg the resolution to "honor the quote." Later drift to 37.5 hours couldn't have inflated it.
  • It forces the escalation. Anything over the $500 solo cap flags for RM/CET sign-off before it's offered. A $2,061 resolution would have required a second set of eyes instead of a solo call under pressure.
  • It separates our failures from day-of conditions. Understaffing and the inventory miss are service issues (a discount is owed); elevator logistics, the particular customer, and clock-milking are delay factors (not a discount to us). Itemizing them bounds the number instead of eating the whole gap.
  • Per-factor hours make the overage legible. Logging how many hours each factor added answers "how much of the 17.5-hour overage is actually ours?" — and produces a defensible figure, not arithmetic off the invoice.
  • The case summary arms the review. Because it clears $500, the auto-summary hands the RM every fact in the moment — instead of the rationale being reconstructed after the money was already committed.
The lesson: Service recovery was the right instinct — the mistake was the shape of the number. Commit a fixed dollar amount you choose, sized to what we actually broke; never a cap that floats with the final bill. And once you're past $500, it's not your call to make alone.
